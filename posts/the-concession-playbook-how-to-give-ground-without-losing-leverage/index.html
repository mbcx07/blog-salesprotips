The Concession Playbook: How to Give Ground Without Losing Leverage

2026-05-22 · Negotiation · Tags: salesprotips, The, negotiation

Every sales professional has been there: the deal is on the table, the buyer leans back, and says, "We need a 10% discount, or we walk." Your heart races. You've invested weeks in this opportunity. The instinct is to cave, to give the discount, to save the deal. But here's the hard truth: that concession doesn't just cost you margin—it signals to the buyer that your initial price was inflated, that your value is negotiable, and that you're more desperate than they are. The result? You win the deal, but you lose the respect, the leverage, and often the profitability that makes the win worthwhile. In my two decades in sales leadership, I've seen more deals die from poorly managed concessions than from any other single mistake. This article is about flipping that script. It's about turning concessions from a reactive, fear-driven giveaway into a strategic, planned tool that actually strengthens your position. Welcome to the concession playbook—where giving less, strategically, wins more.

The Psychology of Concessions: Why Buyers Expect Them

Before you can master concessions, you need to understand why they exist in the first place. Negotiation is a dance of give and take, and buyers are trained to expect movement. It's not just about the money—it's about the feeling of winning. When a buyer asks for a discount, they're not always trying to squeeze your margins; they're testing your confidence and looking for validation that they're getting a good deal. Research in behavioral economics shows that people derive more satisfaction from a deal when they feel they've negotiated something, even if the final price is the same as a fixed offer. This is the anchoring effect combined with the need for control.

But here's the trap: if you give too easily, you devalue your product. If you give too little, you risk losing the deal. The key is to understand that every concession you make is a signal. It tells the buyer something about your product's worth, your company's flexibility, and your own confidence. A well-timed, well-framed concession can actually increase the perceived value of your offer. For example, if you throw in a free training session after a price objection, you're not just giving away something—you're adding a service that addresses a specific need. That's not a discount; it's a value-add. The psychology shifts from "I'm paying less" to "I'm getting more."

So, the first step in mastering concessions is to reframe your mindset. Stop thinking of concessions as losses. Start thinking of them as investments in the relationship and the deal. But that investment must be deliberate, not reactive. You need to know what you're willing to give, what you're not, and what you want in return. That's the foundation of the playbook.

The Reciprocity Trap: How to Use It, Not Fall Into It

Reciprocity is one of the most powerful forces in human interaction. When someone does something for us, we feel an obligation to return the favor. In negotiation, this can work for you or against you. If you make a concession, the buyer feels a subtle pressure to give something back—maybe a larger order, a faster timeline, or a referral. But if you're not careful, you can fall into the trap of making unilateral concessions, giving ground without asking for anything in return. That's not reciprocity; that's surrender.

The professional approach is to use reciprocity deliberately. When you make a concession, always tie it to a request. For example, "I can extend the payment terms to 60 days, but in exchange, I need you to commit to a two-year contract instead of one." This does two things: it makes your concession conditional, and it creates a sense of fairness. The buyer sees that you're not just giving away value—you're trading it. This actually increases their respect for you and your offer.

But beware of the reciprocity trap on the other side. Buyers will often make a small concession early in the negotiation—like agreeing to a meeting or providing a bit of information—and then expect you to reciprocate with a big discount. Don't fall for it. Acknowledge their gesture, but don't overvalue it. A meeting is not a concession; it's a step in the process. You need to calibrate the value of what you give versus what you receive. The best way to do that is to have a clear plan before you enter the negotiation.

Pre-Planning Your Concession Ladder: Know What You Can Give and What You Can't

In my years as a Sales VP, I've seen too many reps walk into a negotiation without a clear idea of what they're willing to concede. They react to every objection with a discount, or they hold firm and risk losing the deal. The solution is to build a concession ladder before you ever sit down at the table. This is a structured list of everything you can offer, ranked from least to most valuable, and what you want in return for each rung.

Start by listing all the potential concessions you might make: price discounts, extended payment terms, additional services, faster delivery, training, support packages, or even non-monetary items like exclusivity or co-marketing. Then, assign a cost to each—not just in dollars, but in terms of your margin, your resources, and your strategic position. Next, decide what you need in return for each concession. This could be a larger order volume, a longer contract, a testimonial, a referral, or a commitment to a pilot program. The key is to make every concession conditional on something that moves the deal forward.

Here's a simple example of a concession ladder:

  • Rung 1 (Lowest value): Offer a free implementation call (cost: 1 hour of your time). In return, ask for a decision by a specific date.
  • Rung 2: Extend payment terms from 30 to 45 days (cost: minor cash flow impact). In return, ask for a larger initial order.
  • Rung 3: Add a premium support package (cost: $500 per month). In return, ask for a 12-month contract instead of month-to-month.
  • Rung 4 (Highest value): Offer a 5% discount (cost: significant margin). In return, ask for a multi-year commitment and a case study.

By pre-planning, you avoid the panic of the moment. You know exactly what you can give and what you need in return. You also know your walk-away point—the rung beyond which you won't go. This clarity gives you confidence, and confidence is leverage.

The Conditional Concession: Never Give Without Getting

The most common mistake in negotiation is making unconditional concessions. You say, "Okay, we can do 10% off," and the buyer says, "Great, and can you also include free shipping?" You've just opened the door to a never-ending list of demands. The fix is to make every concession conditional. This doesn't mean being combative; it means being professional. You're not giving something away; you're trading it.

When you make a conditional concession, you also create a sense of scarcity and value. For example, instead of saying, "We can offer a discount," say, "I can offer a 5% discount if we can agree on a two-year contract and you're willing to provide a testimonial after the first quarter." This does several things: it shows that your discount has value, it ties the discount to a commitment that benefits you, and it gives the buyer a sense of accomplishment—they've earned the discount by meeting your conditions.

Another powerful technique is the "if-then" framing. "If you can increase the order to 500 units, then I can adjust the price." This turns the negotiation into a problem-solving exercise rather than a tug-of-war. You're both working toward a solution that meets both parties' needs. And when you do make a concession, always pause and let the buyer respond. Don't fill the silence. Let them process the value of what you've just offered. This is where the art of the pause comes in—it gives your concession weight.

Framing Your Concessions: How Presentation Changes Perceived Value

You can make the exact same concession in two different ways, and get two completely different reactions. That's the power of framing. For example, instead of saying, "We'll give you a 5% discount," say, "We're willing to invest 5% of our margin to make this partnership work because we believe in the long-term value of this relationship." The first framing is transactional; the second is relational. The buyer feels like you're making a sacrifice for them, which increases their commitment.

Another framing technique is to bundle concessions. Instead of offering a discount alone, offer a package: "I can't reduce the price, but I can include a free training session for your team, extend the warranty to 18 months, and provide a dedicated account manager for the first year." This makes the concession feel more substantial, even if the cost to you is similar. It also gives you more room to negotiate—you can remove one item from the bundle as a counter-concession if needed.

Also, consider the timing of your concessions. The longer you wait to make a concession, the more valuable it appears. If you give in immediately, the buyer thinks you had room to give. If you hold firm for a while and then make a small move, the buyer feels they've won something. This is why patience is a virtue in negotiation. Don't be afraid to say, "I understand your concern, but our pricing is based on the value we deliver. Let me see what I can do, but I'll need your commitment on X." Then, after a pause, make your move.

The Walk-Away Power: How Knowing Your BATNA Strengthens Your Concessions

Finally, the most powerful tool in your concession playbook is your walk-away point. In negotiation theory, this is called your BATNA—Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. If you know exactly what you'll do if this deal falls through, you can negotiate from a position of strength. You're not desperate; you're choosing. And that choice gives you the confidence to make concessions that are strategic, not reactive.

Before you enter any negotiation, write down your BATNA. What are your other options? Can you sell to another prospect? Can you wait for a better opportunity? What is the minimum deal you're willing to accept? This isn't about being negative; it's about being prepared. When you know your walk-away point, you can make concessions that are within your acceptable range, and you can say no to demands that would push you below it. You can also use your BATNA as leverage: "I have another offer on the table, but I'm willing to work with you if we can find a mutually beneficial arrangement." This doesn't mean bluffing—it means being honest about your alternatives.

In my experience, the best negotiators are the ones who are most willing to walk away. They're not attached to the deal; they're attached to the value. And that detachment actually makes them more attractive to buyers. Buyers want to work with people who are confident, not desperate. So, build your BATNA, know your numbers, and let that knowledge guide your concessions. When you do make a concession, it will be a deliberate choice, not a surrender.

Conclusion

Concessions are not a sign of weakness; they're a sign of strategic intelligence. The difference between a salesperson who loses margin and a salesperson who wins deals with healthy margins is not the amount they give—it's the way they give it. By understanding the psychology of concessions, using reciprocity to your advantage, pre-planning your concession ladder, making every concession conditional, framing your offers effectively, and knowing your walk-away power, you can turn the concession game from a liability into a powerful tool. The next time a buyer asks for a discount, don't panic. Smile, refer to your playbook, and make a move that strengthens your position. That's how you give ground without losing leverage—and that's how you close deals that are profitable, sustainable, and respected. Now go out there and negotiate like the professional you are.
