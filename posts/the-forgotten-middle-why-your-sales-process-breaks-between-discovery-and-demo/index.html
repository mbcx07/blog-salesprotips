The Forgotten Middle: Why Your Sales Process Breaks Between Discovery and Demo

2026-07-02 · Strategy · Tags: salesprotips, strategy, The

We obsess over the opening. The perfect cold call script, the ideal first email, the discovery call questions that unlock a prospect's soul. We also obsess over the close. The trial close, the assumptive close, the negotiation tactics that squeeze out the last drop of margin. But between that brilliant first conversation and the final signature, there's a vast, murky territory where deals go to die. I call it the Forgotten Middle. It's the gap between the initial excitement of a qualified lead and the formal demonstration of your solution. In my two decades in sales leadership, I've watched countless reps—and entire teams—lose deals not because they failed to open or close, but because they fumbled the messy, unglamorous work in between. This is the phase where momentum stalls, champions go quiet, and competitors sneak in through the back door. This article is about reclaiming that territory and building a bridge of intentionality across the chasm that separates a promising pipeline from a closed-won deal.

The Hidden Cost of the 'Discovery-to-Demo' Gap

Think of your sales process as a three-act play. Act One is Discovery: you're on stage, asking smart questions, building rapport, and identifying pain points. Act Three is the Close: the grand finale where you present the proposal and ask for the business. But Act Two—the period where you're supposed to be building consensus, tailoring your solution, and creating urgency—is often improvised. And improvisation in sales is rarely profitable.

The cost of this improvisation is staggering. Deals that stall in this phase take 30% to 50% longer to close, if they close at all. The reason is simple: without a structured plan for the middle, your prospect's interest begins to decay. The initial pain point that sparked the conversation gets buried under their daily firefighting. The champion you identified gets distracted by internal politics. The budget you were targeting gets reallocated. You're not losing to a competitor; you're losing to inertia.

To fix this, you must treat the middle as a distinct, deliberate phase with its own objectives and milestones. It's not just a waiting room for the demo. It's the phase where you transform a lead into a partner, and a product pitch into a tailored solution. The goal is not to schedule a demo; the goal is to make the demo a foregone conclusion by the time you walk into it.

The Diagnostic Shift: From 'What' to 'Why'

The most common mistake I see in the Forgotten Middle is a focus on the what rather than the why. Reps spend this time sending product specs, case studies, and white papers. They're answering questions, but they're not asking the right ones. The middle is not for information delivery; it's for diagnostic depth.

Your discovery call gave you the surface-level pain: "Our onboarding process takes too long." The middle is where you dig into the systemic impact of that pain. You need to understand the why behind the what. Why does the long onboarding process matter? Is it causing customer churn? Is it burning out the implementation team? Is it delaying revenue recognition? Each answer reveals a new stakeholder who cares about a different aspect of the problem.

This is where you move from being a vendor to being a consultant. Your conversations in this phase should be structured around questions like:

  • "What has been the financial impact of this issue over the last twelve months?"
  • "Who else in your organization feels this pain most acutely, and how does it affect their goals?"
  • "What have you already tried to solve this, and why did it fail?"
  • "If we could wave a magic wand and fix this today, what would that enable your team to do next?"

These questions do more than gather data. They force the prospect to articulate the value of solving the problem, which builds internal commitment. When a prospect has to explain to you, in detail, why this matters, they are selling themselves on the solution. Your job in the middle is to be the catalyst for that self-discovery.

The Mutual Action Plan: Your Bridge Across the Chasm

If there's one tool that separates elite sales teams from the rest, it's the Mutual Action Plan (MAP). A MAP is a simple, shared document that outlines the steps both you and the prospect will take to get from where you are to a decision. It's not a sales forecast; it's a project plan for the deal. And it's the single most effective way to navigate the Forgotten Middle.

Too many reps treat the sales process as a one-way street. They push, the prospect pulls back. A MAP changes the dynamic. It makes the process collaborative. You're no longer asking, "Can we schedule a demo?" You're saying, "Based on what we've discussed, here are the three key milestones we need to hit to build a business case for your team. The first is a deep-dive demo with your IT team. The second is a security review. The third is a ROI analysis. Which of these makes sense to tackle first?"

This approach does several things. It establishes a cadence of accountability. It surfaces hidden objections early—if the IT team is unavailable, you know there's a potential blocker. It also creates a sense of forward momentum. Every meeting, every deliverable, is a step closer to the finish line. When you present the MAP, you're not just selling a product; you're selling a process. And a process is much harder to say no to than a product.

I've seen reps use MAPs to compress sales cycles by weeks. The reason is that it eliminates the dead time. Instead of waiting for a prospect to "get back to you," you have a pre-agreed schedule of next steps. If they deviate from the plan, it's a red flag that the deal is in trouble—and you can address it head-on rather than letting it fester.

The Art of Strategic Silence and the 'Un-Demo'

In the Forgotten Middle, your mouth should be closed more often than it's open. This is the phase for listening, not talking. But there's a specific type of listening that wins deals: listening for the unspoken. I call it strategic silence. When you ask a tough question—like "What's the real reason you're looking to change vendors?"—you must resist the urge to fill the void. Let the silence hang. The prospect will often answer their own question with a deeper truth than they intended to share.

This leads to a concept I call the 'Un-Demo.' The traditional demo is a monologue where you show off features. The Un-Demo is a dialogue where you co-create the solution. Before you ever open your product, you should have a clear picture of the prospect's ideal outcome. So, in the middle phase, you might say, "I have a rough idea of how our platform could help, but I want to make sure we're aligned. Can you walk me through what a successful implementation looks like to you in 90 days?"

Then, you shut up and listen. You take notes. You ask clarifying questions. When you finally do get to the demo, you're not showing them your product; you're showing them their future. You're mapping your features to their specific outcomes. The demo becomes a validation of their own ideas, not a pitch. This is a subtle but profound shift. When a prospect sees themselves in your solution, the price objection becomes secondary to the value realization.

Leveraging Your CRM to Navigate the Middle

Your CRM is not just a place to log calls and emails. It's the command center for navigating the Forgotten Middle. If you're not using it to track the qualitative aspects of the deal, you're flying blind. Most reps use the CRM to track the what (meetings held, emails sent). The best reps use it to track the why (the emotional drivers, the political landscape, the specific value metrics).

In your CRM, create a custom field for each deal called 'Value Drivers.' In this field, you'll write a sentence or two about what the champion told you they personally want to achieve. For example: "Sarah (CFO) wants to reduce month-end close time from 5 days to 2 days to improve her team's work-life balance." This is gold. When you're in the middle of the deal and you need to re-engage Sarah, you don't send her a generic update. You send her a note that says, "I was thinking about your goal to get your team home by 6 PM on month-end. I found a workflow that could help."

Your CRM should also be used to track the 'Deal Health Score.' This isn't just about deal size or probability. It's a composite score based on the presence of a MAP, the number of stakeholders engaged, and the clarity of the value drivers. If a deal has been in the middle phase for more than two weeks without a MAP, the health score drops. This triggers a coaching conversation, not a panic. The CRM becomes an early warning system, alerting you to deals that are at risk of falling into the void before they actually do.

Conclusion: The Middle Is Where Champions Are Made

The Forgotten Middle is not a problem to be solved; it's an opportunity to be seized. It's where you differentiate yourself from every other rep who's just trying to get to the demo. It's where you build the trust and consensus that makes the close inevitable. By shifting your focus from the opening and closing acts to the critical second act, you transform your sales process from a series of disjointed events into a cohesive, strategic narrative.

Start today by looking at your current pipeline. Identify the deals that are stuck in the middle. Ask yourself: Do they have a Mutual Action Plan? Do I know the personal value drivers of every stakeholder? Am I asking 'why' questions or just sending 'what' documents? The answers will tell you exactly where your revenue is leaking. The middle is not a waiting room. It's the arena. And it's where you'll either lose the deal or win the customer for life.
