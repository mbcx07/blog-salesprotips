The Concession Paradox: Why Giving Ground Too Early Costs You the Deal and the Margin

2026-06-03 · Negotiation · Tags: The, negotiation, salesprotips

There is a moment in nearly every significant negotiation where the air in the room changes. It usually happens about twenty minutes in, after the pleasantries are done and the real numbers are on the table. The buyer leans forward, lowers their voice, and says something like, “Look, we love the product, but we need you to come down 15% or we can’t move forward.” In that instant, your entire strategy is tested. The instinct for most sales professionals is to give a little ground to show good faith, to keep the momentum going, to avoid the awkward silence. But here is the hard truth that I have learned over two decades in sales leadership: the moment you make your first concession is the moment you lose control of the negotiation. It is not a sign of strength; it is a signal of desperation. This article is about the paradox of concessions. We will explore why giving ground too early actually devalues your offering, how to reframe your thinking around give-and-take, and most importantly, how to use a structured approach to concessions that protects your margin while still moving the deal forward. By the end, you will have a new framework for negotiation that treats every concession as a strategic asset, not a peace offering.

The Psychology of the First Move

To understand why early concessions are so damaging, we have to look at the psychology of the buyer. When a procurement officer or a CFO asks for a discount, they are not just trying to save money. They are testing your confidence. They are probing to see how much you value your own product. If you immediately offer a 5% discount, you are telling them that your initial price was inflated. You are confirming their suspicion that there was always room to move. This is the anchoring effect in action. The first number that is put on the table sets the reference point for the entire negotiation. If you move off that anchor quickly, you are not just giving up margin; you are giving up credibility.

I remember a deal early in my career where I was selling a software solution to a mid-sized manufacturing firm. The list price was $120,000, and I had room to go down to $100,000. The buyer opened with a request for a 20% discount, which would have put us at $96,000. I panicked. I wanted to show flexibility, so I countered with $110,000, thinking I was being clever. The buyer didn’t even blink. He just said, “I see. So you can do $110,000. Let me talk to my team.” He walked out of the room, came back ten minutes later, and said, “We’ll do $105,000, but that’s our final offer.” I had lost all leverage. I had shown him that my initial price was a starting point, not a serious number. The deal closed at $105,000, but I learned a painful lesson: the first concession is never a small one. It is a crack in the dam, and the buyer will always push until the whole thing breaks.

Reframing Concessions as Currency, Not Giveaways

The most successful negotiators I have worked with share a common mindset: they view concessions not as losses, but as currency. In any negotiation, you have a basket of items that you can trade. Price is just one of them. There is delivery timeline, payment terms, scope of work, training hours, support levels, and future pricing guarantees. When you treat every one of these as a separate line item, you stop thinking in terms of “how much can I give?” and start thinking in terms of “what am I getting in return?” This is the fundamental shift that separates a salesperson from a strategic negotiator.

Let me give you a concrete example. A few years ago, I was negotiating a large contract with a national retail chain. The buyer wanted a 10% price reduction, which was non-negotiable for us. Instead of saying no, I said, “I can’t do 10% off the list price, but I can offer you a 5% discount if you agree to a 24-month contract instead of 12, and if you’re willing to be a reference account for us.” The buyer paused. He had not expected a counter-offer that was conditional. He came back with a counter: “We’ll take the 5% discount, but we want the 24-month term and we’ll only be a reference if we’re happy after the first quarter.” I agreed, but I added a clause that the reference status was part of the contract. In the end, I gave up 5% in margin, but I gained a two-year commitment and a marketing asset that brought in three more deals that year. The concession was not a giveaway; it was a trade.

The “If-Then” Framework: Making Every Concession Conditional

So how do you operationalize this mindset? The most effective tool I have found is the “If-Then” framework. It is deceptively simple, but it requires discipline. The rule is this: you never make a concession without attaching a condition. If the buyer asks for a discount, you say, “If I can get you a 5% discount, then can you commit to signing by the end of the month?” If they ask for faster delivery, you say, “If I can move your delivery up by two weeks, then can you agree to the standard payment terms instead of net 60?” The “If-Then” structure does two things. First, it slows down the negotiation. It forces the buyer to think about what they are willing to give in return, which makes them more deliberate. Second, it creates a sense of reciprocity. The buyer feels like they are getting something, but they are also giving something up. This is not manipulation; it is the essence of a fair exchange.

Here is where most salespeople fail. They use the “If-Then” framework, but they make the condition too weak. They say, “If I can do this, will you move forward?” That is not a condition; that is a plea. A strong condition is specific, measurable, and time-bound. It is not “will you move forward?” It is “will you sign the contract by Friday?” It is not “will you be happy?” It is “will you agree to a quarterly business review for the first year?” The more specific the condition, the more value you extract from the concession. I have seen deals where a salesperson gave up a 10% discount in exchange for a vague promise of “a long-term partnership.” That is not a concession; that is a donation.

Concession Mapping: Planning Your Walk-Back Before You Walk In

One of the most common mistakes I see in negotiation is improvisation. Salespeople walk into a meeting with a target price in mind, but they have no plan for how they are going to get there. They react to the buyer’s moves instead of executing a strategy. This is where concession mapping comes in. Before you ever sit down at the table, you need to map out your concession ladder. This is a list of every concession you are willing to make, ranked from least costly to most costly, and the condition you will attach to each one. For example, your ladder might look like this: first, you are willing to offer extended payment terms (net 45 instead of net 30) in exchange for a larger initial order. Second, you are willing to include an extra training session at no cost in exchange for a testimonial. Third, you are willing to reduce the price by 3% in exchange for a 24-month contract. Fourth, you are willing to reduce the price by 5% in exchange for a signed contract within 48 hours.

The key is to start at the bottom of the ladder and work your way up slowly. You never jump to the top rung. If the buyer asks for a 5% discount, you do not immediately offer the 3% discount. You start with the payment terms. You say, “I can’t do 5% off, but I can offer you more favorable payment terms.” If they push back, you move to the next rung. This process does two things. It makes the buyer work for every inch of progress, which increases their commitment to the deal. And it ensures that you never give away more than you need to. I have seen negotiations where the buyer was willing to accept a 2% discount, but the salesperson offered 5% right off the bat because they were nervous. Concession mapping prevents that. It gives you a script to follow, so you are not making decisions in the heat of the moment.

The Power of the Non-Monetary Concession

In my experience, the most underutilized tool in a salesperson’s arsenal is the non-monetary concession. We get so fixated on price that we forget that buyers care about a host of other things. They care about risk. They care about internal politics. They care about implementation effort. They care about how the decision will make them look to their boss. If you can address these concerns, you can often avoid touching the price at all. For example, if a buyer is hesitant because they are worried about the implementation disrupting their team, you can offer to provide a dedicated implementation manager for the first month. That costs you very little, but it has enormous value to the buyer. If a buyer is worried about making a mistake, you can offer a pilot program or a proof of concept. Again, this is a concession, but it is not a price cut.

I recall a deal with a healthcare provider where the sticking point was not price, but data migration. The buyer was terrified that moving from their legacy system to ours would cause downtime and data loss. They asked for a 10% discount to “compensate for the risk.” Instead of discounting, I offered to have our professional services team handle the entire migration at no cost, and I included a service level agreement that guaranteed 99.9% uptime during the transition. The buyer was thrilled. They got the risk mitigation they wanted, and I protected my margin. The deal closed at full price. The lesson here is simple: before you cut your price, ask yourself what else the buyer might value. Often, the answer is something that costs you far less than a discount.

Conclusion

The concession paradox is this: the more willing you are to give, the less the buyer values what you are selling. Every time you give ground without a fight, you are telling the buyer that your product was overpriced to begin with. The solution is not to be stubborn or inflexible. It is to be strategic. Start by reframing concessions as currency, not giveaways. Use the “If-Then” framework to make every concession conditional. Map out your concession ladder before you enter the room, and always look for non-monetary concessions that address the buyer’s deeper fears and desires. When you do this, you will find that you close more deals, protect your margins, and earn the respect of your buyers. They may not always get the discount they asked for, but they will get a solution that works, and you will get a deal that is profitable. That is the art of negotiation, and it is a skill that will serve you for your entire career.
