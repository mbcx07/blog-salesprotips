The Buyer's Journey Is a Lie: How to Sell the Way Your Customers Actually Decide

2026-05-29 · Strategy · Tags: The, salesprotips, strategy

For decades, we've been sold a fairy tale. The narrative goes something like this: a prospect identifies a problem, conducts methodical research, evaluates three or four vendors against a rigid checklist, and then makes a logical, well-informed decision. This is the classic linear buyer's journey—Awareness, Consideration, Decision. It's clean, it's predictable, and it's almost entirely fictional. In my twenty years in sales leadership, I've watched countless reps lose deals not because their product was inferior, but because they were playing chess on a board where the customer was playing a completely different game. The modern buyer isn't taking a journey; they're navigating a chaotic, emotional, and often illogical maze. They're influenced by a late-night podcast, a panicked email from their CFO, a competitor's slick LinkedIn ad, and a gut feeling about a sales rep they met at a conference six months ago. If you're still building your sales process around the outdated notion of a linear journey, you're not just behind the times—you're actively losing revenue. This article is about discarding that comfortable lie and embracing a new, messier, and far more profitable reality: the Decision Lattice.

The Funnel Fallacy: Why Linear Thinking Fails

The traditional sales funnel is a beautiful piece of abstract art. It suggests that if you just pour enough leads in the top, a predictable percentage will trickle out the bottom as closed won deals. This model assumes that buyers move smoothly from one stage to the next, and that our job as salespeople is simply to nudge them along. But this model has a fatal flaw: it ignores the human element. Buyers don't progress; they regress. They get excited, then they get scared. They build consensus, then a new stakeholder throws a wrench in the works. They have a great demo, then they read a bad review and go dark for two weeks.

I remember a deal from my early days as an AE. I had a champion, a VP of Operations, who was completely sold. We had passed every technical evaluation, the ROI spreadsheet was glowing, and the legal review was a formality. According to my CRM, this deal was a lock. It was in the 'Negotiation' stage, and I was already mentally spending my commission. Then, out of nowhere, the deal died. What happened? The VP's CEO had a golf game with a board member from a competitor. That one casual conversation, based on zero technical merit, killed a six-month sales cycle. The funnel didn't predict that. It couldn't. The buyer didn't take a journey; they took a detour off a cliff.

To win in this environment, we must abandon the funnel as a predictive tool and embrace it only as a descriptive one. It's a useful way to track historical data, but it's a terrible way to forecast future behavior. The real buying process is a series of loops, jumps, and reversals. Your sales process must be flexible enough to handle that. Instead of asking, "What stage is this deal in?" ask, "What is the buyer feeling right now?" and "What is the political landscape inside their company?" These are the variables that actually determine the outcome.

Introducing the Decision Lattice: A New Framework for Complexity

If the funnel is a lie, what's the truth? I propose we replace the linear journey with a framework I call the Decision Lattice. A lattice is a structure of intersecting points, and that's exactly how modern B2B buying works. The buyer isn't moving from point A to point B. They are bouncing between different nodes of influence: the economic buyer, the end-user, the IT security team, the procurement department, and even external voices like industry analysts and peers on Reddit. Each of these nodes has its own agenda, its own timeline, and its own emotional triggers.

Your job as a sales professional is not to push the buyer along a path. It's to map the lattice. You need to identify every single node that will touch this decision, understand the connections between them, and then strategically insert yourself into those intersections. This is a fundamental shift in mindset. Instead of being a tour guide, you become a cartographer. You're not leading the buyer; you're helping them navigate their own internal landscape.

For example, let's say you're selling a new CRM. The VP of Sales is your champion, but the real power might lie with the Head of IT, who is terrified of data migration issues. The lattice shows a direct connection between the VP of Sales and the Head of IT. If you only focus on the VP, you're missing a critical node. You need to equip your champion with the technical ammunition to win over IT. You need to provide a sandbox environment, a detailed security whitepaper, and a migration plan that addresses their specific fears. You're not just selling a CRM; you're selling a solution to the IT leader's anxiety. By mapping the lattice, you can anticipate these needs before they become roadblocks.

Emotional Velocity: The Hidden Metric That Predicts Wins

We love to talk about deal velocity—how quickly a deal moves through the pipeline. But there's a more important, and often ignored, metric: emotional velocity. This is the speed at which the buyer's emotional commitment to your solution increases. A deal can have high deal velocity (moving through stages quickly) but low emotional velocity (the buyer is just going through the motions). These are the deals that fall apart at the finish line. Conversely, a deal can have slow deal velocity but high emotional velocity. The buyer is taking their time, but they are becoming more and more convinced that you are the only choice. These are the deals that survive the inevitable storms.

How do you measure emotional velocity? You can't put it in a CRM field, but you can feel it. It's in the tone of the buyer's emails. It's in the questions they ask. It's in the people they introduce you to. A buyer with high emotional velocity will start selling for you. They'll say things like, "I've been thinking about our conversation, and I think we should also consider..." They'll proactively share internal documents. They'll defend you in meetings you're not in.

To increase emotional velocity, you need to stop focusing on features and benefits and start focusing on personal and professional wins. Ask questions like:

  • "What does this project mean for your career?"
  • "If this goes perfectly, how will you be recognized?"
  • "What's the personal risk if you choose the wrong solution?"

These questions tap into the emotional core of the decision. They move the conversation from a transactional level to a personal one. When a buyer feels that you understand their personal stakes, their emotional velocity skyrockets. They're no longer buying a product; they're buying a path to their own success, and you're the guide who can get them there.

Strategic Disorganization: The Power of the 'Un-Demo'

We've all been trained to deliver a polished, scripted demo. We have our slides, our talking points, and our carefully choreographed feature walkthroughs. But in a world where buyers have seen a thousand demos, the scripted approach is a liability. It signals that you're a salesperson, not a partner. It creates a power dynamic where you are the performer and they are the audience. And audiences are passive. Passive buyers don't buy.

Instead, I want you to consider the power of the 'Un-Demo.' This is a session where you deliberately introduce a little bit of chaos. You don't show them your standard deck. You ask them to bring their own data, their own workflows, and their own problems. Then, you build a solution on the fly. You say, "I have no idea if our tool can do this, but let's try it right now." This vulnerability is incredibly disarming. It shows that you're not hiding anything. It proves that your product is flexible enough to handle their unique needs.

I once watched a rep win a million-dollar deal by doing this. He spent the first twenty minutes of the demo trying to import a messy, real-world CSV file that the prospect had brought. It failed. Twice. The prospect looked nervous. But the rep just laughed and said, "Well, that's why we have a great support team. Let's look at the error log and figure this out together." They spent the next hour debugging the file, and in the process, they built a deeper level of trust than any polished demo could have achieved. The prospect saw that the rep was a problem-solver, not a pitch-man. The 'Un-Demo' is about showing your work. It's about letting the buyer see the messy, human process of solving their problem. That's what builds unshakeable confidence.

Redefining the Win: How to Sell to the 'No'

We are conditioned to see 'No' as a failure. We're taught to overcome objections, to push through resistance, and to never take 'No' for an answer. This is a toxic and expensive mindset. In the complex lattice of modern buying, a 'No' is often the most valuable piece of information you can get. It's not a rejection; it's a data point. It's a signal that you've hit a node in the lattice that you haven't properly mapped.

When a buyer says 'No,' they are usually saying one of three things: 'No, not right now,' 'No, not with you,' or 'No, I don't see the value.' Each of these requires a different response. 'No, not right now' is a timing issue. You need to understand the trigger that will change their mind. 'No, not with you' is a trust or fit issue. You need to understand why they don't see you as a viable partner. 'No, I don't see the value' is a communication issue. You haven't connected your solution to their emotional or business drivers.

I want you to start a practice of 'selling to the No.' This means actively encouraging the buyer to voice their doubts and concerns early and often. Ask them, "What would have to be true for you to walk away from this deal?" or "What's the biggest reason you might not move forward?" This is terrifying because it invites negativity. But it's also liberating. It allows you to address the real objections, not the surface-level ones. When you sell to the 'No,' you stop wasting time on deals that are never going to close, and you dramatically increase your win rate on the deals that can. You're not just accepting rejection; you're using it as a strategic tool to filter your pipeline and focus your energy on the highest-probability opportunities.

Conclusion: Embrace the Chaos, Reap the Rewards

The linear buyer's journey is a comfortable myth. It makes us feel like we have control. But in the real world, buying is a chaotic, emotional, and deeply human process. The sooner you accept this, the sooner you can start winning. Stop trying to force your buyers into a pre-defined funnel and start mapping their unique Decision Lattice. Stop obsessing over deal velocity and start cultivating emotional velocity. Stop delivering polished performances and start having messy, authentic conversations. And stop fearing the 'No' and start using it as a strategic compass. This isn't just a new set of tactics; it's a new philosophy. It's a shift from being a peddler of products to being a navigator of complex decisions. It's harder, it's less predictable, and it requires a lot more emotional intelligence. But for those who are willing to embrace the chaos, the rewards are immense. You'll not only close more deals, but you'll build deeper, more resilient relationships with your customers—relationships that will survive the inevitable ups and downs of the business world. The funnel is dead. Long live the lattice.
